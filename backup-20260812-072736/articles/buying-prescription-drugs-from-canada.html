Buying Prescription Drugs from Canada: What's Legal, Safe, and Worth It

Savings Guide July 30, 2026 7 min read By Matt Overeem, Technology Assessment Project LLC

Every year, millions of Americans look north for relief from sky-high prescription costs. Canadian pharmacies can offer savings of 50% to 80% on many brand-name medications. But the legal landscape is murky, safety concerns are real, and not every online "Canadian pharmacy" is what it claims to be.

The Legal Reality: It's Complicated

Let's start with the part most people get wrong. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, it is technically illegal for individuals to import prescription drugs from Canada or any other country into the United States. The law is clear on paper. But the practice on the ground is very different.

The FDA has maintained an "enforcement discretion" policy for decades. In practical terms, this means the agency generally does not take action against individuals who import small quantities of medications for personal use. The unofficial guidelines allow for a supply of up to 90 days, require that the medication is for a serious condition, and expect that you have a valid U.S. prescription.

How rarely does enforcement happen? Customs and Border Protection seizes less than 0.1% of personal pharmaceutical imports. When packages are flagged, they're typically sent a warning letter and the medication is destroyed, not the recipient prosecuted. No American has been prosecuted for importing a personal supply of medication from a licensed Canadian pharmacy.

State Importation Programs
Several states have gone further than just looking the other way. Florida, Colorado, Vermont, New Hampshire, and others have passed laws creating formal state importation programs. Florida's program, authorized under a provision of the Medicare Modernization Act, allows wholesalers to import certain drugs from Canada with state oversight. While implementation has been slow due to federal approval requirements, these programs signal a growing acceptance of cross-border purchasing.

The Safety Question: How to Protect Yourself

The biggest risk of buying from Canada isn't the law. It's the possibility of getting counterfeit or substandard medication from a pharmacy that isn't actually Canadian at all. Hundreds of websites claim to be Canadian pharmacies but are actually based in countries with little pharmaceutical regulation.

Your safety depends entirely on verifying who you're buying from. The single most important step is to use a pharmacy certified by the Canadian International Pharmacy Association (CIPA). As of 2026, CIPA has certified 51 pharmacies that meet rigorous standards for safety, legitimacy, and proper licensing. You can verify any pharmacy at cipa.com.

Here is a safety checklist to follow before ordering:

Verify CIPA certification. Go to cipa.com and search for the pharmacy by name. If it's not listed, don't use it. Period.

Check that they require a valid prescription. Any legitimate Canadian pharmacy will require a prescription from a licensed physician. If a site offers to sell you prescription drugs without one, it's a red flag that should end your consideration immediately.

Confirm the pharmacy has a Canadian license. Real Canadian pharmacies are licensed by their provincial regulatory body. You can verify licenses through the relevant provincial pharmacy college, such as the Ontario College of Pharmacists or the College of Pharmacists of British Columbia.

Look for a physical Canadian address and phone number. Call the number. A real pharmacy will have pharmacists available to answer questions.

Check the medication when it arrives. Verify the packaging, markings, and appearance match the expected manufacturer. If anything looks off, don't take it.

What You Can and Cannot Import

Not everything available in a Canadian pharmacy can be imported into the United States, even under the FDA's discretionary approach. Understanding these limits protects you from legal risk and safety issues.

Generally OK to Import (Personal Use, 90-Day Supply)
Standard brand-name and generic oral medications, common maintenance drugs (statins, blood pressure medications, thyroid drugs, antidepressants), and non-controlled prescription medications that are also approved in the U.S.
Do NOT Import
Controlled substances (opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants) are strictly prohibited and will be seized. Biologic medications (insulin is an exception in some cases, but most biologics cannot be safely shipped). REMS drugs (medications under Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies due to serious safety concerns). IV medications and injectables other than certain well-established products.

How Much Can You Actually Save?

The savings from Canadian pharmacies are most dramatic on brand-name medications that don't have generic alternatives in the United States. Canada's Patented Medicine Prices Review Board caps the prices drug makers can charge, resulting in prices that are typically 40% to 70% lower than U.S. retail.

For generic medications, the savings are less compelling. U.S. generic prices have dropped significantly, and after factoring in shipping costs of $10 to $25 per order, Canadian generics may not be cheaper. The sweet spot for Canadian purchasing is brand-name drugs with no U.S. generic available.

The Ordering Process

Ordering from a CIPA-certified Canadian pharmacy is similar to using a U.S. mail-order pharmacy, with a few differences. You'll select your medication on the pharmacy's website and provide your U.S. prescription. Most pharmacies accept prescriptions by fax, mail, or digital transfer from your doctor's office. The pharmacy will have a licensed Canadian pharmacist review your prescription before dispensing.

Payment is usually by credit card in Canadian dollars. Your bank will handle the currency conversion. Shipping to the U.S. typically takes 10 to 21 business days via international mail. Some pharmacies offer express shipping for an additional fee, which can reduce delivery time to 7 to 10 days.

Plan ahead. Unlike driving to your local pharmacy, you can't get a Canadian order filled the same day. Most experienced buyers order their refills 3 to 4 weeks before they run out to account for shipping times and potential customs delays.

When Canadian Purchasing Doesn't Make Sense

Cross-border purchasing isn't the right solution for everyone or every medication. If you need a medication urgently, the shipping timeline makes it impractical. If your drug is a generic that's already affordable in the U.S., the savings may not justify the effort. If you have good insurance with low copays, your out-of-pocket cost may already be lower than Canadian cash prices. And if your medication requires cold chain shipping or careful handling, the extended transit time introduces risk.

Before ordering from Canada, always check domestic prices first. Use RxGator to compare U.S. pharmacy prices with available coupons and discount programs. In many cases, a U.S. discount price or manufacturer coupon can match or beat Canadian prices without the wait.

The Bottom Line

Buying from Canadian pharmacies is a legitimate savings strategy for Americans paying high cash prices for brand-name drugs. The legal risk to individuals is extremely low, the savings can be substantial, and safety is manageable if you stick to CIPA-certified pharmacies. But it's not a blanket solution. Do your homework, verify the pharmacy, understand what you can and can't import, and always compare against domestic alternatives before placing an order.
