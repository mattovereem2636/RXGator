How Much Does Gabapentin Cost Without Insurance in 2026?

Gabapentin is the ninth most prescribed medication in the United States, used for everything from nerve pain to seizures. As a well-established generic, it can be very affordable — but the price depends heavily on your dosage and where you fill it.

What Gabapentin Actually Costs at the Pharmacy

Gabapentin is an anticonvulsant medication originally developed to treat epilepsy, but it is now far more commonly prescribed for nerve pain (neuropathy), restless legs syndrome, and as an adjunct for certain types of chronic pain. It has been available as a generic since 2004, when the patent on the brand-name version, Neurontin, expired.

The pricing for gabapentin is a bit more variable than other common generics because of dosage. Gabapentin is typically prescribed at higher doses than most medications — 300 mg taken three times daily is a common starting dose, and many patients end up on 600 mg or 800 mg three times daily. That means a 30-day supply can be 90 capsules or more, which affects the total cost.

For the most common prescription — 90 capsules of gabapentin 300 mg (a 30-day supply at three times daily) — the retail cash price without insurance typically runs $50 to $80 at chain pharmacies. With a free discount card from GoodRx, SingleCare, or RxSaver, that same prescription drops to $6 to $15. The wholesale cost per capsule is around $0.05, so the retail markup is enormous.

Higher dosages cost more simply because you are buying more medication. A 30-day supply of gabapentin 800 mg three times daily (90 tablets) runs $10 to $25 with a discount card, or $70 to $120 at full retail price.

Key point: Gabapentin prices depend on both the dosage strength and the quantity per month. A discount card can save you $40 to $100 per fill — always use one.

Generic Gabapentin vs. Brand-Name Neurontin

Gabapentin was originally sold as Neurontin by Pfizer (via its acquisition of Warner-Lambert/Parke-Davis). The brand version is still technically available, but there is absolutely no reason to fill it. Generic gabapentin contains the same active ingredient, in the same dosage, and meets the same FDA standards.

Brand-name Neurontin can cost $700 to $900 or more for a 30-day supply at typical dosages. Generic gabapentin with a discount card runs $6 to $25 for the same prescription. The savings are staggering — easily $800 or more per month — and the medications are clinically identical. Your pharmacist will automatically substitute the generic unless your prescription specifically says "dispense as written."

Gabapentin vs. Pregabalin (Lyrica)

Gabapentin and pregabalin (brand name Lyrica) are closely related medications that work similarly for nerve pain and seizures. Pregabalin is sometimes prescribed when gabapentin does not work well enough or when the three-times-daily dosing of gabapentin is inconvenient (pregabalin is typically twice daily).

Generic pregabalin has become available and affordable, but it still tends to cost more than gabapentin — typically $15 to $30 for a 30-day supply with a discount card, compared to $6 to $15 for gabapentin. If your doctor is considering switching you to pregabalin, it is worth comparing the actual prices at your pharmacy. In many cases, the dosing convenience of pregabalin may not justify the extra cost, especially if gabapentin is working well for you.

Where to Find the Lowest Price

Free discount card programs offer the most consistent savings on gabapentin. GoodRx, SingleCare, and RxSaver all show prices of $6 to $15 for a 30-day supply of gabapentin 300 mg (90 capsules) at major chains including CVS, Walgreens, Kroger, and Walmart.

Walmart's $4 generic list includes gabapentin, though the price depends on the dosage and quantity. A 30-day supply of lower doses may qualify for the $4 or $9 price, while higher doses or larger quantities will cost more. It is worth checking the specific price for your prescription.

Cost Plus Drugs offers gabapentin at near-wholesale pricing, typically $4 to $8 for a 30-day supply of the 300 mg capsules plus a flat dispensing and shipping fee. For patients on higher doses, the savings compared to retail can be substantial — potentially $100 or more per month.

Mail-order pharmacies and 90-day fills can also bring the per-month cost down significantly. A 90-day supply with a discount card often runs $15 to $30, which works out to $5 to $10 per month.

Important note: Gabapentin is classified as a controlled substance (Schedule V) in some states, which may affect which pharmacies and discount programs can fill it. Check your state's regulations and confirm with your pharmacy that they accept discount cards for controlled substances.

Insurance Copays vs. Cash Price

Gabapentin is placed on Tier 1 (preferred generic) by most insurance plans, with copays of $5 to $15. Since discount card prices often fall in a similar range, the savings from paying cash are less dramatic than with some other generics. However, it is still worth comparing both prices at your pharmacy — a $6 discount card price beats a $15 Tier 1 copay by $9 every month, which adds up to over $100 per year.

As with all generic medications, keep in mind that insurance copays count toward your deductible and out-of-pocket maximum, while cash payments do not. If you have multiple expensive prescriptions and are working toward your deductible, running gabapentin through insurance might make strategic sense even if the copay is a few dollars more.
