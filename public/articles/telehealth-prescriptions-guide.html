How to Get Prescriptions Through Telehealth in 2026

Virtual doctor visits have become a mainstream way to get prescriptions — but there are important rules, limits, and cost considerations you should know before booking your first appointment.

How Telehealth Prescriptions Work

A telehealth visit follows the same basic framework as an in-person appointment, just conducted over video, phone, or sometimes asynchronous messaging. You connect with a licensed healthcare provider, describe your symptoms or discuss a condition, and the provider makes a clinical decision about treatment. If a prescription is appropriate, the provider sends it electronically to the pharmacy of your choice — the same e-prescribing system used by brick-and-mortar doctors' offices.

The prescription itself is no different from one written during an in-person visit. It carries the same legal authority, uses the same electronic transmission standards, and can be filled at any licensed pharmacy in your state. You are not locked into using a specific pharmacy or mail-order service associated with the telehealth platform, although some platforms will steer you toward their own pharmacy partners. You always have the right to choose where you fill your prescription, and exercising that right can save you real money.

Telehealth has become particularly popular for managing ongoing conditions like hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, anxiety, depression, allergies, and skin conditions. For these types of prescriptions — where the provider is managing a known condition rather than diagnosing something new — a virtual visit is often just as effective as sitting in a waiting room, and far more convenient.

What Can and Cannot Be Prescribed Online

Most non-controlled medications can be prescribed through telehealth without restrictions. This includes antibiotics for straightforward infections, blood pressure medications, statins, SSRIs and other antidepressants, birth control, inhalers, topical treatments, and many other common prescriptions. If your condition can be reasonably evaluated through a conversation and a review of your medical history, a telehealth provider can prescribe for it.

Watch out: Controlled substances have stricter rules for telehealth prescribing. Under the DEA's updated telehealth regulations, providers can prescribe Schedule III through V controlled substances (such as certain sleep aids, testosterone, or medications for ADHD like non-stimulant options) via telehealth under specific conditions. However, Schedule II controlled substances — including stimulants like Adderall and opioid painkillers — generally require at least one in-person evaluation before a telehealth provider can prescribe or continue prescribing them. These rules have evolved since the pandemic-era flexibilities, so check current DEA guidance if you need a controlled medication.

Some conditions also require hands-on evaluation that telehealth simply cannot provide. If a provider needs to listen to your lungs, perform a physical exam, or run lab work to make a diagnosis, they will likely recommend an in-person visit. Good telehealth providers are transparent about these limitations and will refer you to in-person care when the clinical situation warrants it.

What Telehealth Visits Cost

The cost of a telehealth visit varies widely depending on the platform, your insurance status, and the type of visit. Direct-to-consumer telehealth platforms typically charge between $25 and $75 for a basic visit without insurance. Some subscription-based services offer unlimited visits for a monthly fee, usually in the range of $15 to $30 per month. These can be a good value if you need frequent check-ins or medication management.

If you have health insurance, telehealth visits are broadly covered. Most commercial insurance plans, Medicare, and Medicaid programs cover telehealth with the same cost-sharing as in-person visits — meaning you pay your normal copay, which might be $20 to $40 for a primary care visit. Some plans have waived telehealth copays entirely or offer $0 telehealth as an incentive to reduce unnecessary emergency room and urgent care visits. Check your specific plan benefits before booking.

When calculating the true cost of a telehealth visit, factor in the savings beyond the visit fee itself. There is no gas, parking, or transit cost. There is minimal time away from work — most telehealth visits last 10 to 20 minutes, with no waiting room time. For parents, there is no need to arrange childcare. These indirect savings often make telehealth the more economical choice even when the visit fee is similar to an in-person copay.

Filling Your Telehealth Prescription at the Best Price

Here is where many people leave money on the table. Some telehealth platforms bundle prescriptions with their service, offering to ship medications directly to you through their affiliated pharmacy. While this can be convenient, it is not always the cheapest option. The platform's pharmacy may charge more than your local pharmacy with a discount card, or more than a competing mail-order service.

Key point: When your telehealth provider asks which pharmacy to send your prescription to, you do not have to decide on the spot. Ask them to send it to whichever pharmacy offers the best price. Before your visit, use RxGator to search your medication and compare prices across pharmacies near you and mail-order options. Then give the provider the pharmacy with the lowest price for your specific drug and dosage.

If the telehealth platform automatically sends the prescription to their own pharmacy, you can usually request a transfer. Call the pharmacy where you want to fill the prescription and ask them to initiate a transfer — most pharmacies handle this routinely and it typically takes less than a day. The only exception is that some controlled substance prescriptions may have transfer restrictions depending on state law.

Getting the Most Out of a Telehealth Visit

Preparation makes the difference between a productive telehealth appointment and a frustrating one. Before your visit, gather a list of your current medications (including doses), note any allergies, and write down your symptoms or questions. If you have recent lab results or records from another provider, have them available to share. Many platforms let you upload documents before the visit, which gives the provider time to review your history.

Be direct about cost concerns. Tell the provider if you are paying out of pocket, if you have a high deductible, or if you need the most affordable option. Good providers will consider cost when choosing between therapeutically equivalent medications. There is often a $200-per-month drug and a $15-per-month drug that do essentially the same thing, and your provider can only prescribe the cheaper one if they know cost matters to you.

Finally, confirm that the provider is licensed in your state. Telehealth prescribing requires the provider to hold a valid license in the state where the patient is located at the time of the visit. Most major telehealth platforms handle this automatically by matching you with appropriately licensed providers, but it is worth confirming — an invalid prescription due to licensing issues will not be filled by any pharmacy.
