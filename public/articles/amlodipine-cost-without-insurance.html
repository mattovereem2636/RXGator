How Much Does Amlodipine Cost Without Insurance in 2026?

Amlodipine is the fifth most prescribed medication in the United States, and as a long-established generic, it is one of the cheapest blood pressure drugs available. Here is what you should actually expect to pay, and where to find the best deals.

What Amlodipine Actually Costs at the Pharmacy

Amlodipine is a calcium channel blocker prescribed for high blood pressure and certain types of chest pain (angina). It has been available as a generic since 2007 when Pfizer's patent on the brand-name version, Norvasc, expired. Since then, dozens of manufacturers have entered the market, pushing prices down to pennies per pill at the wholesale level.

If you walk into a pharmacy without insurance and ask for a 30-day supply of generic amlodipine, you can expect to pay anywhere from $3 to $45 depending on the pharmacy, your dosage, and whether you use a discount card. The most common dosages are 2.5 mg, 5 mg, and 10 mg, and the price difference between them is usually negligible since the manufacturing cost is essentially the same regardless of strength.

At the low end, discount card programs from GoodRx, SingleCare, and RxSaver routinely bring the price to $3 to $6 for a 30-day supply at major chain pharmacies. At Walmart and other retailers with $4 generic programs, amlodipine is typically listed at $9 for a 30-day supply and $24 for a 90-day supply. Costco's pharmacy, which does not require a membership in most states, also offers competitive pricing in the $4 to $8 range.

At the high end, walking into CVS or Walgreens without any discount card and paying the retail cash price can run $30 to $45 for the same 30-day supply. That markup is not because the medication is expensive to produce — it is the pharmacy's retail pricing, and it is exactly the kind of gap that makes price comparison essential.

Key point: With a free discount card, amlodipine can cost as little as $3 for a 30-day supply. Without one, the same pharmacy might charge $40 or more. Always check before you pay.

Generic Amlodipine vs. Brand-Name Norvasc

Amlodipine was originally sold as Norvasc by Pfizer, and the brand name is still technically available. However, there is almost never a reason to fill the brand version. Generic amlodipine contains the same active ingredient, in the same dosage, and meets the same FDA standards for quality, purity, and bioavailability.

The cost difference is enormous. Brand-name Norvasc can run $400 or more for a 30-day supply without insurance, compared to under $10 for the generic. Unless your doctor has specifically written "dispense as written" on the prescription — which is extremely rare for amlodipine — your pharmacist will automatically substitute the generic. If you ever see a surprisingly high price at the register, the first thing to ask is whether you are getting the generic version.

Where to Find the Lowest Price

For most people, the cheapest option is a free discount card used at a major chain pharmacy. GoodRx, SingleCare, and RxSaver all show amlodipine prices in the $3 to $6 range at pharmacies like CVS, Walgreens, Kroger, and Rite Aid. These cards work like a coupon — no enrollment, no insurance required, and they are accepted at virtually every pharmacy in the country.

Retailer pharmacy programs are another strong option. Walmart's $4 generic list includes amlodipine at $9 for 30 days and $24 for 90 days. While that is slightly higher than the best discount card prices, it requires no card or app — you simply present your prescription and ask for the program price.

Mark Cuban's Cost Plus Drugs offers amlodipine at very competitive prices, typically $3 to $5 for a 30-day supply plus a flat dispensing and shipping fee. The medication is shipped directly to your door, which is convenient if you do not have a cheap pharmacy nearby.

For the best deal on a 90-day supply, compare prices carefully. Some discount cards offer 90 tablets for $8 to $12, which works out to less than $3 per month. If you are on a stable dose and take amlodipine daily, filling 90 days at a time is almost always the smarter financial move.

Insurance Copays vs. Cash Price

Here is something worth knowing: your insurance copay for amlodipine might actually be higher than the cash price with a discount card. Many insurance plans place generics on Tier 1 with copays of $5 to $15. If a discount card gets you the same medication for $3, paying cash is the better deal.

The one consideration is your deductible. If you are trying to meet your annual deductible or out-of-pocket maximum, running prescriptions through insurance counts toward those thresholds, while cash payments typically do not. For a medication this inexpensive, the difference is usually not significant, but it is worth thinking about if you have other costly prescriptions during the year.

Watch out: Some pharmacies automatically bill your insurance even when the cash price is lower. Ask your pharmacist to compare both options and use whichever saves you more.
