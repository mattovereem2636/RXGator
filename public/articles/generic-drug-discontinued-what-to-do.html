What Happens When a Generic Drug Gets Discontinued?

Drug shortages and manufacturer exits can leave you scrambling. Here's what to do when your generic disappears and how to find alternatives fast.

You go to pick up your prescription and the pharmacist tells you it's out of stock — and not just temporarily. Your generic drug has been discontinued by its manufacturer, or there's a nationwide shortage. It's unsettling, but it happens more often than you'd think, and there are clear steps to take.

Why Generics Get Discontinued

Generic drug manufacturing is a low-margin business. When a generic is produced by multiple manufacturers, competition drives prices down — sometimes to the point where it's no longer profitable for some companies to keep making it. Manufacturers exit the market, and if enough leave, the remaining supply can't meet demand.

Other causes include manufacturing quality issues (the FDA can shut down production lines that fail inspections), raw material shortages (many generic drug ingredients come from a small number of global suppliers), regulatory changes that increase production costs, and natural disasters or geopolitical disruptions affecting supply chains.

The FDA maintains a public drug shortage database that tracks current shortages and estimated resolution timelines. Your pharmacist can also check industry databases for real-time availability information.

What to Do Immediately

Don't stop taking your medication abruptly — for many drugs, sudden discontinuation can cause withdrawal effects or a return of symptoms. Call your doctor's office and explain the situation. They can prescribe a therapeutic alternative — a different medication in the same drug class that treats the same condition. For most conditions, multiple drugs exist in the same class, so an alternative is usually available.

Ask your pharmacist to check availability at other nearby pharmacies. A shortage at one location doesn't always mean a shortage everywhere — different pharmacies use different wholesalers, and one may still have stock. Your pharmacist can also check whether a different manufacturer's version of the same generic is available.

Check multiple sources: When a drug is short at retail pharmacies, mail-order and online pharmacies sometimes still have supply. Cost Plus Drugs, Amazon Pharmacy, and other direct-to-consumer pharmacies source independently and may have inventory when local pharmacies don't. Longer-Term Adjustments

If a shortage persists, your doctor may need to adjust your treatment plan. This could mean switching to a different drug in the same class, switching to a brand-name version of the same drug (which may cost more but is usually still available during generic shortages), adjusting the dose of an available alternative, or in some cases, compounding the medication through a specialty pharmacy.

If you switch to a new medication, your doctor will likely want to monitor you more closely during the transition to make sure the alternative works as well. Keep communication open and report any changes in how you feel.

Prevention for Next Time

For critical medications, keep a buffer supply. Ask your doctor to prescribe a 90-day supply and refill it when you have 30 days remaining — that way you always have a cushion if supply disruptions occur. Know the names of therapeutic alternatives to your key medications so you can discuss options quickly if a shortage hits. And stay informed — sign up for FDA drug shortage notifications or ask your pharmacist to alert you if supply issues emerge for your medications.
