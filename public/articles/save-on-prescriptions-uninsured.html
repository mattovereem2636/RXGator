5 Ways to Save on Prescriptions if You're Uninsured

No insurance doesn't mean full price. These five strategies can cut your prescription costs by 50% to 90% — starting today.

About 27 million Americans don't have health insurance, and roughly one in four adults reports skipping medications because of cost. If you're paying full retail for prescriptions, you're almost certainly overpaying. The pharmacy counter price is not the real price — it's just the starting point for negotiation, and there are at least five ways to negotiate it down dramatically.

1. Use Free Discount Cards

GoodRx and SingleCare offer free prescription discount cards that can reduce generic drug prices by 50% to 80% at most pharmacies. You don't need insurance, you don't need to sign up for an account, and you can use them immediately — just pull up the coupon on your phone and show it to the pharmacist.

These aren't insurance. They're pre-negotiated discount rates that pharmacies have agreed to honor. The pharmacist runs the discount instead of an insurance claim, and you pay the reduced cash price. Always check both GoodRx and SingleCare for the same drug, because their prices vary by pharmacy.

2. Fill at the Cheapest Pharmacy

Pharmacy pricing is not regulated. The same generic drug can cost $4 at Walmart and $45 at an independent pharmacy in the same shopping center. Consistently cheap options for generics include Walmart ($4 list covering about 300 drugs), Costco (no membership required for the pharmacy — by law in most states), and Publix and Meijer (which offer select generics for free in participating states).

Cost Plus Drugs is Mark Cuban's mail-order pharmacy that sells generics at the manufacturer's actual cost plus a flat 15% markup and a $5.25 shipping fee. For maintenance medications where you can wait 5-7 business days for delivery, it's often the absolute cheapest option.

3. Ask About Manufacturer Assistance Programs

If you take an expensive brand-name drug, the manufacturer almost certainly has a patient assistance program (PAP) for uninsured patients. These programs provide free or deeply discounted medications to people who meet income guidelines — and the income limits are often more generous than you'd expect.

You can find manufacturer programs by searching your medication on the drug company's website, calling the number on the drug's packaging, or searching NeedyMeds.org or RxAssist.org, which aggregate assistance programs across manufacturers.

4. Ask Your Doctor About Generics and Therapeutic Alternatives

If your doctor prescribes a brand-name drug, ask whether a generic equivalent exists. For many common conditions, generic alternatives work identically to the brand version at a fraction of the cost. If no generic exists for your specific medication, ask whether a different drug in the same therapeutic class has a generic available.

Doctors don't always know what drugs cost at the pharmacy counter, so don't assume they've factored price into their prescribing decision. A simple conversation can sometimes save you hundreds of dollars per month.

5. Look into Community Health Centers and Assistance Programs

Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) serve patients regardless of insurance status or ability to pay, and many have 340B pharmacy programs that offer medications at deeply discounted prices. You can find a community health center near you at findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov.

State pharmaceutical assistance programs (SPAPs) exist in many states and provide medication help to residents who don't qualify for Medicaid but can't afford prescriptions. Your state's health department website will list available programs.
