State Prescription Assistance Programs: Free or Low-Cost Drugs by State

Savings Guide July 30, 2026 6 min read By Matt Overeem, Technology Assessment Project LLC

Millions of Americans qualify for state-run prescription assistance but never apply because they don't know these programs exist. More than 30 states operate pharmaceutical assistance programs that can provide free or deeply discounted medications to eligible residents.

What Are State Pharmaceutical Assistance Programs (SPAPs)?

State Pharmaceutical Assistance Programs, commonly called SPAPs, are state-funded programs that help residents afford their prescription medications. Unlike federal programs such as Medicare Part D or Medicaid, SPAPs are designed, funded, and administered at the state level. That means eligibility rules, covered medications, and benefit levels vary significantly from one state to another.

Most SPAPs were created to fill gaps left by federal programs. They typically target seniors aged 65 and older, people with disabilities, and low-income residents who earn too much to qualify for Medicaid but still struggle with drug costs. Some states limit their programs to specific conditions like HIV/AIDS or cancer, while others cover a broad range of medications.

The key thing to understand is that these programs exist specifically because legislators recognized that federal safety nets have holes. If you fall into one of those holes, your state may have a program built just for your situation.

Major State Programs Worth Knowing About

While every SPAP is different, several stand out for their scope and generosity. These examples illustrate the range of what's available.

New York EPIC (Elderly Pharmaceutical Insurance Coverage)
Covers New Yorkers aged 65+ with incomes up to $75,000 (single) or $100,000 (married). Members pay a quarterly fee and reduced copays. EPIC coordinates with Medicare Part D, covering costs in the coverage gap and helping with premiums. Over 300,000 New Yorkers use this program.
New Jersey PAAD (Pharmaceutical Assistance to the Aged and Disabled)
Available to residents 65+ or receiving Social Security disability benefits with income under approximately $28,000 (single) or $34,000 (married). Members pay copays as low as $5 for generics. PAAD also covers Medicare Part D premiums for qualifying members.
Pennsylvania PACE/PACENET
Two-tier program for residents 65+. PACE covers those with income under $14,500 (single) or $17,700 (married) with copays of $6-$9. PACENET extends coverage to incomes up to $27,500 (single) or $35,500 (married) with slightly higher cost-sharing. Together, they serve over 260,000 Pennsylvanians.
California ADAP (AIDS Drug Assistance Program)
While disease-specific, California's ADAP is one of the largest in the country, providing HIV/AIDS medications to residents with income up to 500% of the federal poverty level. Every state has an ADAP program funded in part by the federal Ryan White program.

How SPAPs Work with Medicare Part D

If you're enrolled in Medicare Part D, an SPAP can be a powerful complement. Many SPAPs are designed to coordinate directly with Part D plans. Here's how the coordination typically works:

The SPAP may pay your Part D premium, reducing your monthly costs. During the coverage gap (the "donut hole"), the SPAP can cover your out-of-pocket costs, effectively eliminating the gap's financial impact. SPAP spending counts toward your Part D True Out-of-Pocket (TrOOP) costs, meaning it helps you reach catastrophic coverage faster. Some SPAPs also cover medications that your Part D plan excludes from its formulary.

This coordination is by design. When Medicare Part D launched in 2006, many states restructured their SPAPs to work as "wrap-around" programs rather than standalone benefits. The result is that having both Part D and an SPAP can dramatically reduce your total prescription costs.

How to Find Your State's Program

Finding out whether your state offers a pharmaceutical assistance program takes a few minutes of research. Here are the most reliable resources:

Medicare.gov: The official Medicare website maintains a list of SPAPs organized by state. Search for "State Pharmaceutical Assistance Programs" on the site, or call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) and ask a representative to check your state's offerings.

BenefitsCheckUp.org: Run by the National Council on Aging, this free tool lets you enter your age, income, location, and medications to find all assistance programs you may qualify for, including SPAPs, manufacturer programs, and other benefits.

Your State's Department of Health or Aging: Every state health department website lists available assistance programs. Search "[your state] prescription assistance program" to find the most current information.

Your local Area Agency on Aging: These agencies can help you navigate the application process and identify programs you may not find on your own. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to find your local agency.

Eligibility: Who Qualifies?

Eligibility requirements vary widely by state, but most SPAPs consider three primary factors: age or disability status, income level, and state residency.

Age requirements typically start at 65, though some states set lower thresholds or include people receiving Social Security Disability Insurance regardless of age. Income limits range from as low as $14,000 to as high as $75,000 or more for individuals, depending on the state and program tier. Nearly all programs require you to be a legal resident of the state.

Some states also require that you be enrolled in Medicare Part D, while others serve as alternatives for people who don't have Part D or any prescription coverage at all. A few states have no age requirement and base eligibility entirely on income and medical need.

The Biggest Problem: People Don't Know They Qualify
Studies consistently show that many eligible Americans never apply for SPAPs simply because they don't know the programs exist. A 2024 analysis estimated that fewer than half of eligible seniors are enrolled in their state's pharmaceutical assistance program. If you're struggling with drug costs and live in a state with an SPAP, there is a real chance you're leaving money on the table.

How to Apply

The application process for most SPAPs is straightforward. You'll typically need to provide proof of age (birth certificate or government ID), proof of state residency (utility bill, lease, or driver's license), proof of income (tax return, Social Security statement, or pension documentation), a list of your current prescriptions, and your Medicare or insurance information if applicable.

Most states accept applications online, by mail, or in person at local offices. Processing times vary from a few days to several weeks. Some states, like New York, allow provisional enrollment so you can start receiving benefits while your application is processed.

Stacking SPAPs with Other Savings

SPAPs can often be combined with other cost-reduction strategies for maximum savings. Manufacturer copay cards may work alongside SPAP benefits for brand-name drugs. Patient assistance programs from drug makers can supplement SPAP coverage for expensive specialty medications. Discount programs like those found through RxGator can help with drugs not covered by your SPAP.

The general rule is to use your SPAP as your foundation and layer additional savings on top. An SPAP doesn't restrict you from seeking lower prices elsewhere for medications it doesn't cover or for situations where another option provides a better deal.

What If Your State Doesn't Have an SPAP?

Not every state runs a comprehensive pharmaceutical assistance program. If yours doesn't, you still have options. Check for disease-specific programs like ADAP, which exist in all 50 states. Look into manufacturer patient assistance programs, which are available nationwide. Use RxGator to compare cash prices at local pharmacies, which can sometimes beat insurance copays. Explore Medicare Extra Help (Low Income Subsidy) if you're a Medicare beneficiary. Contact 211 or your local United Way to find community-based prescription assistance.
