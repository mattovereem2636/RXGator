90-Day Prescriptions: Why Buying in Bulk Saves Money

Filling a 90-day supply instead of monthly can save 20% to 40% on many medications — plus fewer pharmacy trips.

If you take a medication daily — blood pressure, cholesterol, thyroid, diabetes, antidepressant — you're probably filling a 30-day supply every month. Switching to a 90-day supply can save you 20% to 40% on the same medication, plus cut your pharmacy trips from twelve per year to four.

Why 90-Day Fills Are Cheaper

The savings come from multiple sources. Many pharmacies price 90-day supplies at less than three times the 30-day price — Walmart, for example, charges $10 for a 90-day supply of drugs on its discount list versus $4 per month ($12 for three months at retail). Insurance plans often offer 90-day fills at two copays instead of three, especially through mail-order pharmacies. And pharmacies save labor by filling one large prescription instead of three small ones, which they pass on as lower per-unit pricing.

The math is simple: If your 30-day copay is $10, three monthly fills cost $30 per quarter. A 90-day fill through your plan's mail-order pharmacy might cost $20. That's $40 saved per year on just one medication. If you take three maintenance drugs, the annual savings add up to over $100.

How to Switch

You'll need a new prescription from your doctor — a 30-day prescription can't be automatically converted to 90-day fills. Call your doctor's office and ask them to write or e-prescribe a 90-day supply with refills. Most doctors are happy to do this for stable, long-term medications. The pharmacy needs the prescription to explicitly authorize the 90-day quantity.

If you use insurance, check whether your plan offers better pricing on 90-day fills through its mail-order pharmacy. Some plans require mail-order for 90-day supplies, while others allow it at retail. Call your plan's pharmacy benefit number or check your plan documents.

When 90-Day Fills Make Sense

The 90-day approach works best for medications you've been on for a while with stable dosing. If your doctor is still adjusting your dose or monitoring for side effects, stick with 30-day fills until the medication is stabilized. You don't want 90 days' worth of a drug sitting in your cabinet if your doctor decides to change the dose or switch medications entirely after the first month.

It also doesn't make sense for medications you take "as needed" or for short-term prescriptions like antibiotics. And some controlled substances may not be available in 90-day supplies depending on your state's regulations.
