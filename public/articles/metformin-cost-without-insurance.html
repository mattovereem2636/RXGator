How Much Does Metformin Cost Without Insurance in 2026?

Generic metformin runs $4 to $30 per month depending on your pharmacy. Here's where to find the lowest price and what most people actually pay.

Metformin is the most commonly prescribed oral medication for Type 2 diabetes in the world, and it's also one of the cheapest. But "cheap" depends entirely on where you fill your prescription. The retail price at one pharmacy might be $30 while the store two miles away charges $4 for the exact same pill.

If you're paying out of pocket, here's what metformin actually costs in 2026 — and how to make sure you're not overpaying.

What Most People Actually Pay

Generic metformin 500 mg immediate-release tablets — the most common starting dose — cost between $4 and $20 for a 30-day supply at major retail pharmacies. A 60-tablet supply (the typical monthly quantity at 500 mg twice daily) averages around $10 to $30 without insurance or discount cards.

With a discount card from GoodRx or SingleCare, that price drops to as low as $6 to $9 at most pharmacies. And at Walmart, Publix, or Meijer, you can get metformin on their $4 generic drug lists — no card, no coupon, no insurance needed.

Bottom line: No one should be paying more than $15 per month for generic metformin in 2026. If you are, you're filling at the wrong pharmacy or not using available discounts.

The immediate-release (IR) version is the most affordable. Extended-release (ER) costs slightly more but is still very affordable as a generic. The brand-name versions (Glumetza, Fortamet) cost dramatically more with no clinical advantage for most patients — your doctor can almost always prescribe the generic.

Why Prices Vary So Much

The same 60 tablets of generic metformin can cost $4 at Walmart and $30 at an independent pharmacy across the street. This happens because pharmacies negotiate their own wholesale prices with distributors, set their own retail markups, and face different overhead costs depending on location and volume.

Chain pharmacies with high volume typically offer more competitive generic pricing. Independent pharmacies may charge more because their acquisition costs are higher and they can't leverage the same bulk purchasing power.

Your location matters too. Prescription prices tend to run higher in major metro areas like Los Angeles compared to cities like Atlanta, even at the same pharmacy chain.

How to Get the Lowest Price

The single most effective step is to compare prices across pharmacies before you fill. This is exactly what RxGator does — we pull pricing from 12 different sources so you can see your options in one search.

Beyond that, these strategies work for metformin specifically. First, ask about the Walmart $4 list: metformin IR is one of the roughly 300 generics available for $4 per 30-day supply or $10 for 90 days at any Walmart or Sam's Club pharmacy. No membership, no coupon, no insurance required — just ask the pharmacist to ring it as a cash transaction.

Second, check Publix and Meijer, which offer free metformin in some states as part of their generic drug programs. Third, try a 90-day supply — buying three months at once usually saves 20% to 40% compared to monthly fills, and your pharmacist can work with your doctor to adjust the prescription. Finally, consider Cost Plus Drugs: Mark Cuban's transparent pharmacy sells generic metformin at acquisition cost plus a flat 15% markup, with $5 shipping.

Pro tip: Even if you have insurance, compare your copay to the cash price with a discount card. For many generic drugs including metformin, the cash price with a GoodRx or SingleCare coupon is lower than your insurance copay. The catch: paying cash doesn't count toward your deductible.

What About Insurance Coverage?

Nearly all insurance plans — including Medicare Part D, Medicaid, and commercial plans — cover generic metformin as a Tier 1 preferred generic. Most insured patients pay $0 to $5 per month. If your plan is charging you more than $10 for generic metformin, check your formulary or call member services — something may be wrong with how the claim is processing.

The extended-release versions have spottier coverage. About half of commercial and ACA marketplace plans cover metformin ER generics, and only about one in five Medicare plans do. If you're prescribed the ER version and your insurance doesn't cover it well, ask your doctor whether the IR version would work for you — it's the same active ingredient, just taken more frequently.
