Compounding Pharmacies: Custom Medications and When They Make Sense

Education July 30, 2026 6 min read By Matt Overeem, Technology Assessment Project LLC

Not every patient fits into the one-size-fits-all world of manufactured medications. When a standard drug doesn't work because of the dose, the form, or an ingredient, compounding pharmacies can create a custom version tailored to your specific needs. Here's what you need to know before going the compounding route.

What Is Compounding?

Compounding is the practice of creating customized medications by combining, mixing, or altering ingredients to meet an individual patient's needs. Unlike manufactured drugs that come off a production line in fixed doses and forms, compounded medications are made one prescription at a time by a pharmacist or pharmacy technician.

Compounding has been part of pharmacy practice since the profession began. Before mass pharmaceutical manufacturing, every prescription was compounded. Today, an estimated 1% to 3% of all prescriptions in the United States are compounded, representing a market worth roughly $15 billion. While most people will never need a compounded medication, for those who do, it can be the difference between having a workable treatment and having none at all.

When Compounding Makes Sense

There are several legitimate scenarios where compounding is the best or only option for a patient. Understanding these helps you determine whether compounding is right for your situation.

Discontinued medications. When a manufacturer stops making a drug that you've been stable on for years, a compounding pharmacy can often recreate it. Drug discontinuations happen regularly, sometimes because a drug isn't profitable enough, not because it doesn't work. If your medication disappears from the market, compounding may be your best path to continuity.

Allergies to inactive ingredients. Commercial medications contain inactive ingredients like dyes, preservatives, lactose, gluten, and various fillers. If you're allergic or sensitive to one of these ingredients, a compounding pharmacy can make the same active medication without the problematic component.

Pediatric doses. Many medications aren't manufactured in doses appropriate for children. A child who needs a fraction of an adult dose, or who can't swallow a pill, may need a compounded liquid formulation in a child-friendly flavor. This is one of the most common reasons for compounding.

Alternative delivery forms. If you can't swallow pills, a compounding pharmacy can turn an oral medication into a liquid, a topical cream, a suppository, or even a lozenge. Patients with swallowing difficulties, nausea, or conditions that prevent oral administration benefit from this flexibility.

Combination medications. If you take multiple medications for the same condition, a compounding pharmacy can sometimes combine them into a single preparation. This simplifies your regimen and can improve adherence, especially for patients managing multiple chronic conditions.

Compounding Is Not the Same as Generic Manufacturing
An important distinction: compounding pharmacies create medications for individual patients based on specific prescriptions. They are not manufacturing generic versions of brand-name drugs for general sale. Every compounded medication requires a valid prescription from a licensed healthcare provider for a specific patient.

Two Types of Compounding Facilities

Federal law distinguishes between two categories of compounding operations, and understanding the difference matters for safety.

503A Pharmacies are traditional compounding pharmacies that prepare medications based on individual patient prescriptions. They are regulated primarily by state boards of pharmacy. These are your local compounding pharmacies, and they make medications one prescription at a time. They cannot compound drugs in large batches or sell to hospitals and clinics without patient-specific prescriptions.

503B Outsourcing Facilities are larger operations that can compound medications in bulk without patient-specific prescriptions. They supply hospitals, clinics, and other healthcare facilities. In exchange for this broader authority, 503B facilities are subject to FDA oversight, including current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) requirements and regular inspections. These facilities exist largely to address drug shortages by producing medications that manufacturers can't or won't supply.

For individual patients, you'll almost always be dealing with a 503A pharmacy. The 503B distinction matters primarily for the healthcare facilities that purchase from them.

The GLP-1 Compounding Controversy

The explosion in demand for GLP-1 medications like semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) has thrust compounding pharmacies into the national spotlight and created significant controversy.

Why Compounded GLP-1s Are Cheaper
When a brand-name drug is on the FDA's drug shortage list, compounding pharmacies can legally make copies of it. With Ozempic costing over $1,000 per month and compounded semaglutide available for $150 to $400 per month, the price difference has driven enormous demand. However, this is only legal while the shortage designation remains in place.

The situation with compounded GLP-1 medications is evolving rapidly. The FDA has periodically added and removed semaglutide and tirzepatide from the shortage list, creating uncertainty about whether compounding these drugs is currently legal. When a drug comes off the shortage list, compounding pharmacies must stop making it, and patients who rely on the compounded version face a sudden supply disruption.

Safety concerns are also real. Several compounded GLP-1 products have been recalled due to sterility issues, and the FDA has issued warnings about compounded versions that may not contain the correct dose or formulation. If you're considering compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide, verify that the pharmacy is PCAB-accredited, confirm the drug is currently on the FDA shortage list, understand that purity and potency may not match the brand-name product, and discuss the risks with your prescribing physician.

What Compounding Costs

Compounded medications are typically not covered by insurance. Most health plans exclude compounded drugs from their formularies because the medications aren't FDA-approved products. That means you'll usually pay cash.

Costs vary widely depending on the ingredients, complexity of the formulation, and the pharmacy. As a general guideline, simple compounded medications like custom-dose capsules or flavored liquids typically cost $30 to $60 per month. Moderate-complexity formulations such as topical creams or combination medications run $50 to $100 per month. Complex preparations including sterile injectables or specialized delivery forms can cost $100 to $300 or more per month.

Some insurance plans will cover compounded medications with prior authorization if the prescriber documents that no commercially available alternative is appropriate. It's worth submitting the claim even if you expect a denial, because some plans do pay, and the denial gives you a basis for appeal.

How to Find a Legitimate Compounding Pharmacy

Quality varies significantly among compounding pharmacies. The most important credential to look for is accreditation from the Pharmacy Compounding Accreditation Board (PCAB), a service of the Accreditation Commission for Health Care. PCAB-accredited pharmacies have undergone rigorous evaluation of their processes, facilities, and quality controls.

Beyond accreditation, look for pharmacies that employ pharmacists with specialized compounding training, maintain proper facilities with clean rooms and quality testing equipment, perform potency and sterility testing on their products, are transparent about their ingredients and sourcing, and have established relationships with prescribers in your area.

You can search for PCAB-accredited pharmacies at achc.org. Your doctor or specialist may also be able to recommend compounding pharmacies they trust and have worked with before.

Questions to Ask Before Compounding

Before committing to a compounded medication, have a conversation with both your doctor and the compounding pharmacy. Ask your doctor whether a commercially available alternative exists that could work. Ask the pharmacy about their quality testing procedures, how long the compounded medication remains stable, proper storage requirements, and what happens if the medication doesn't work as expected. A good compounding pharmacy will be forthcoming about these details and will work with your prescriber to adjust the formulation if needed.

Check Standard Drug Prices First

Before exploring compounding, verify what commercially available versions of your medication cost at local pharmacies. A standard generic may be both cheaper and more convenient.
