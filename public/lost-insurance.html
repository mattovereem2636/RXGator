Immediate Medication Needs (Today)

Emergency Pharmacy Fills

Most chain pharmacies will provide a 3–7 day emergency supply of maintenance medications without a new prescription. Call your pharmacy and ask. Walgreens, CVS, and Rite Aid all have emergency supply policies.

How it works: Most states allow pharmacists to dispense a 72-hour emergency supply of non-controlled medications when your prescriber can't be reached. Some states allow more. Call your pharmacy and say: "I lost my insurance and I'm about to run out of [medication]. Can I get an emergency fill?"

Check If You Qualify for Free or Low-Cost Coverage

Medicaid (Free Coverage — Apply Year-Round)

Available year-round — no enrollment window. If your income dropped when you lost your job, you may now qualify. Medicaid covers prescriptions with little or no copay.

Check eligibility at HealthCare.gov or your state's Medicaid site.

COBRA — Should You Keep It?

COBRA lets you keep your old employer insurance for up to 18 months, but you pay the full premium — both your share and what your employer used to cover, plus a 2% admin fee.

Average COBRA cost in 2026: $584–$900/month for an individual, $1,800–$2,400/month for a family.

When COBRA makes sense: You're mid-treatment with a specialist in your plan's network, you're pregnant, your ACA options are more expensive, or you need specific network access. Otherwise, an ACA Marketplace plan is almost always cheaper.

Get Your Medications Free or Near-Free

Patient Assistance Programs (PAPs)

Drug manufacturers give away their medications to people without insurance who meet income guidelines. Pfizer, Lilly, AbbVie, Novo Nordisk, Merck, AstraZeneca — almost every major company has one. Most programs accept patients at 200–600% of the Federal Poverty Level.

Community Health Centers (FQHCs)

Over 14,000 Federally Qualified Health Center sites across the country serve patients on a sliding fee scale — regardless of insurance status. Through the 340B Drug Pricing Program, medications cost 25–50% less than retail. If your income is at or below the Federal Poverty Level, medications may be free.

Your Long-Term Strategy

Switch to Generics

9 out of 10 prescriptions in the US are filled with generics. If you're on a brand-name drug, ask your doctor about generic alternatives. The savings can be 80–95%. The FDA requires generics to have the same active ingredient, strength, dosage form, and route of administration as the brand.

Use Free Discount Cards

Keep a free discount card on your phone — GoodRx, SingleCare, or RxSaver. The cheapest card changes by drug and pharmacy. RXGator searches them all so you don't have to check each one.

Pro tip: Always compare the discount card price to the pharmacy's cash price. Sometimes the cash price is lower, especially at Costco.

Buy 90-Day Supplies

Buying a 90-day supply instead of monthly saves 20–40% on most medications. Ask your doctor to write the prescription for 90 days. Cost Plus Drugs, Rx Outreach, and most pharmacies offer this option.

Talk to Your Doctor About Cost

Your doctor doesn't always know what your medication costs out of pocket. Tell them you're uninsured and ask about the cheapest effective option. They can often switch you to a lower-cost alternative in the same drug class that works just as well.
