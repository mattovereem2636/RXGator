How to Get Prescriptions Filled While Traveling

Running out of medication on the road doesn't have to be a crisis. Here's how to get prescriptions filled away from home — domestically and internationally.

You're on vacation, on a business trip, or visiting family across the country — and you realize you're about to run out of a medication. Or your luggage with your pills got lost. It feels like a crisis, but it usually isn't. Here's how to get your prescriptions filled away from home.

Domestic Travel: Same Chain Pharmacies

If you use a chain pharmacy (CVS, Walgreens, Walmart, Rite Aid), your prescription records are accessible at any location nationwide. Walk into any branch, give them your name and date of birth, and they can pull up your prescription history and fill a refill if one is available. This is the simplest option and works immediately.

If your prescription is at an independent pharmacy or a chain that isn't nearby, any pharmacy can initiate a transfer. Give the new pharmacy your medication name, dose, and the phone number of your home pharmacy. They'll handle the rest.

No Refills Left? Emergency Supply

Many states have laws or regulations allowing pharmacists to dispense an emergency supply of maintenance medications — typically a 3 to 7 day supply — without a new prescription from your doctor. This applies to non-controlled medications for chronic conditions (blood pressure, thyroid, cholesterol, etc.). The pharmacist will need to verify your prescription history, which they can usually do through their pharmacy network or by calling your home pharmacy.

For controlled substances, emergency fills are more restricted and vary significantly by state. Call ahead to the pharmacy and explain your situation — the pharmacist can tell you what's possible under their state's regulations.

Your doctor can help remotely. Call your doctor's office and ask them to phone in or e-prescribe to a pharmacy near your current location. Most doctors can send prescriptions to any pharmacy in the country. If it's after hours, many practices have on-call physicians who can handle urgent prescription needs.

International Travel

Filling U.S. prescriptions abroad is more complicated. Most countries don't honor prescriptions written by U.S. doctors. If you need medication while traveling internationally, you may need to see a local doctor who can write a local prescription. Travel insurance that covers medical care can help with the cost. Pharmacies in many countries sell common medications without a prescription that would require one in the U.S.

The better approach is preparation. Before international trips, fill a 90-day supply of all your medications. Keep them in original pharmacy-labeled containers (important for customs). Carry a written list of your medications with generic names (brand names vary by country), doses, and your doctor's contact information. And check the drug regulations of your destination country — some common U.S. medications are restricted or banned in certain countries.

Before You Leave: Preparation Checklist

Count your pills and make sure you have enough for the entire trip plus a few extra days. Carry medications in your carry-on luggage, never in checked bags. Bring a copy of your prescriptions or a printout from your pharmacy showing your current medications. If you use a mail-order pharmacy, adjust your delivery dates so a shipment doesn't arrive to an empty house while you're away. And make sure you know how to reach your doctor's office from wherever you'll be.
