7 Things to Do in the First 48 Hours After Losing Insurance

You just lost coverage. The clock is ticking on some deadlines — but panic is not on the to-do list. Here is exactly what to do first.

Whether it happened because of a layoff, a divorce, aging off a parent’s plan, or a job change that didn’t line up quite right — losing health insurance feels like the ground dropped out. If you take prescriptions, that feeling is even worse. Suddenly a medication that cost you $15 has a sticker price of $400, and you’re not sure what to do next.

Take a breath. You have more options than you think, but some of them are time-sensitive. The next 48 hours matter. Here are seven things to do right now, in roughly the order you should tackle them.

1. Don’t Panic-Buy Prescriptions at Full Retail Price

This is the most expensive mistake people make in the first 24 hours. You walk into the pharmacy, hand over your prescription, and the pharmacist says, “That’ll be $387.” Your instinct is to just pay it because you need the medication. Don’t.

Cash prices for the same drug can vary by 80% or more between pharmacies — sometimes within the same zip code. A generic that costs $312 at one chain might be $28 at a nearby competitor or through a mail-order pharmacy. Discount programs, manufacturer coupons, and pharmacy-specific pricing tiers all create massive price gaps that are invisible until you compare.

If you have a few days’ worth of medication left, use that time to shop around. Even if you’re completely out, ask the pharmacist to hold the prescription while you compare — they will.

2. Call Your Pharmacy About Emergency Fills

Most states have laws that allow pharmacists to dispense an emergency supply of maintenance medications — typically a 72-hour to 7-day supply — without a current prescription or active insurance. This exists specifically for situations like yours.

Call your pharmacy and say: “I just lost my insurance and I’m going to run out of [medication name]. Can you do an emergency fill?” Pharmacists handle this regularly. They can often dispense enough to keep you going while you sort out your next steps.

Key detail: Emergency fill rules vary by state. Some states allow up to a 30-day emergency supply for certain medications. Your pharmacist will know the rules for your state. If one pharmacy says no, try another — some pharmacists are more familiar with the process than others.

For controlled substances, emergency fill rules are stricter, but options still exist. Ask your pharmacist or call your prescriber’s office — they may be able to provide samples or adjust your prescription to a lower-cost alternative.

3. File for Unemployment (If Applicable)

If you lost insurance because you lost your job, file for unemployment benefits immediately — even if you think you might not qualify. Here’s why this matters beyond the paycheck:

Filing for unemployment creates a documented record of your job loss, which you’ll need for other benefits. In many states, receiving unemployment can automatically qualify you for Medicaid or reduce your expected income for ACA marketplace subsidies. It also starts the clock on your benefits — most states have a one-week waiting period before payments begin, so every day you delay is a day you wait longer.

You can file online in every state. It typically takes 15–30 minutes. Do it today.

4. Check Medicaid Eligibility Immediately

This is the step most people skip because they assume they won’t qualify. But Medicaid income limits are higher than many people think, and there is no open enrollment period — you can apply any day of the year.

2026 Medicaid income limits (in expansion states):
Individual: up to $22,025/year (138% of the Federal Poverty Level)
Family of 2: up to $29,823/year
Family of 4: up to $45,419/year

If your income dropped because you lost your job, you may now qualify even if you didn’t before.

In the 40 states (plus D.C.) that have expanded Medicaid, any adult under 65 who meets the income threshold can enroll. Medicaid coverage is comprehensive and typically has zero or very low prescription copays — often $1–$4 per medication.

Apply at medicaid.gov or through your state’s health department website. Many states process applications within a few days, and coverage can be retroactive to the date you applied.

5. Start Your 60-Day ACA Special Enrollment Period Clock

Losing employer-sponsored health insurance is a “qualifying life event” that triggers a 60-day Special Enrollment Period on the ACA marketplace. This is your window to buy a marketplace plan outside of the normal open enrollment season — and it starts the day your coverage ends.

Go to HealthCare.gov (or your state’s marketplace if your state runs its own) and create an account. You don’t have to pick a plan today, but you need to know this deadline exists. If you miss the 60-day window, you may have to wait until the next open enrollment period — which could be months away.

Important: When you apply, you’ll enter your expected income for the year. If your income has dropped, your subsidies may be substantial. Many people qualify for plans that cost $0–$50 per month with low prescription copays. Don’t assume you can’t afford it until you see the numbers.

6. Make the COBRA Decision Carefully

If you had employer-sponsored insurance, you’ll likely receive a COBRA notice offering to continue your exact same plan. Sounds great — until you see the price.

Coverage Type Typical COBRA Cost (2026) Typical ACA Silver Plan
Individual $584–$900/month $0–$350/month (with subsidies)
Family $1,600–$2,400/month $0–$800/month (with subsidies)

With COBRA, you pay the full premium your employer used to subsidize — plus a 2% administrative fee. For most people, an ACA marketplace plan with income-based subsidies is significantly cheaper for comparable coverage.

COBRA does have one advantage: it keeps your exact same plan, which means no disruption to your provider network or formulary. If you’re mid-treatment with a specialist, that continuity might be worth the extra cost for a month or two. But for most people, the ACA marketplace is the better deal. You have 60 days to elect COBRA retroactively, so you can compare your options before committing.

7. Look Into Patient Assistance Programs

If you take brand-name or specialty medications, Patient Assistance Programs (PAPs) can be a lifeline. Most major pharmaceutical manufacturers offer programs that provide free or deeply discounted medications to people who are uninsured or underinsured.

Eligibility is usually based on income, and the thresholds are more generous than you might expect — typically 200–600% of the Federal Poverty Level, which means individuals earning up to $95,000+ may qualify for some programs.

Where to find PAPs:

NeedyMeds (needymeds.org) — searchable database of over 1,500 programs. Free to use.
RxAssist (rxassist.org) — another comprehensive database of patient assistance programs.
Manufacturer websites — search “[drug name] patient assistance program” to find the manufacturer’s application directly.

The catch: PAP applications can take 2–6 weeks to process. That’s why steps 1 and 2 matter — you need a bridge strategy for your prescriptions while you wait. Use RxGator to find the lowest cash price now, and apply for PAPs as a longer-term solution.

The Bottom Line

Losing insurance is stressful, but it’s not a dead end. The most important thing you can do in the first 48 hours is protect two things: your immediate prescription access and your future coverage options. Don’t overpay at the pharmacy, don’t miss your enrollment deadlines, and don’t assume you have fewer options than you do.

You can handle this. Start with step one, and work your way down the list.
